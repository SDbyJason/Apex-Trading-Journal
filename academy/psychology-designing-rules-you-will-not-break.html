Designing rules you will not break

Foundation3 min read · part of The psychology of trading

A rule you break weekly is not a discipline problem. It is a design problem, and the fix is to redesign the rule rather than to try harder — the first chapter said this, and this one is about how.

Rules fail for three reasons, and they need different repairs. Some are unenforceable: they depend on a judgement you have to make in the moment you are least able to make it. "I will not trade when I am emotional" cannot work, because the state it names is the state that decides whether it applies. Replace it with something countable — a trade cap, a time window, a loss limit.

Some are too strict for how you actually trade. A rule that forbids something you genuinely need forces a choice between breaking it and trading badly, and the rule loses. If you keep overriding a rule for good reasons, the rule is wrong.

And some have no consequence. A rule that nothing enforces is a preference. This is why a limit in the platform beats a limit in a notebook, and why any constraint you can waive by clicking past a warning provides roughly nothing.

A workable rule has three properties: it is countable, so you can tell afterwards whether you kept it; it is decided in advance, so the stressed version of you never gets a vote; and it fails closed, meaning that when you ignore it something actually stops. Test a new rule against those three before you adopt it, and check after a month how often you kept it. A rule you kept 95% of the time is working. One you kept 60% of the time is telling you something.

What to take away

  • Unenforceable rules depend on judgement in the moment judgement fails — replace with countable ones.
  • A rule you override for good reasons is the wrong rule.
  • A rule nothing enforces is a preference.
  • Countable, decided in advance, fails closed. Then measure how often you actually kept it.

Which of these is unenforceable as written?
I will not trade when I am emotional — It depends on you assessing your own state at exactly the moment that assessment is unreliable. The other three are countable after the fact by anyone.
You override the same rule regularly and each time there was a genuinely good reason. What follows?
The rule does not match how you actually need to trade and should be redesigned — Repeated overrides for sound reasons are evidence about the rule, not about you. A rule that forces a choice between breaking it and trading badly always loses.
What does "fails closed" mean for a trading rule?
When you ignore it, something actually stops rather than merely warning you — A warning you can click past has no enforcement, which makes the rule a preference. The constraint has to act without needing your agreement in the moment.
